Selected work / Consumer research and data

How students use social media, and what it may cost them

A survey of university students on social media use, sleep, well-being and academic performance.

Academic research. Group of three, Business Research course, Canadian University Dubai. A convenience sample from one university, so the results describe these respondents and cannot be generalised.

What I did

I created the survey, analysed the responses, built the graphs and charts, and analysed them.

Method

A quantitative, cross-sectional online survey (Microsoft Forms) of 23 questions on use, study habits, well-being and sleep, analysed in Excel.

Sample

The response file has 63 rows, but two are blank, which leaves 61 complete responses (33 women, 28 men), matching the group report. A few questions have skipped answers, so each chart uses that question's valid responses. Ages run from 18 to over 26, across all years of study plus postgraduates.

Descriptive findingAlmost half of respondents (47%) spend more than three hours a day on social media, and 72% use it often or always before sleep.
Statistical results, checked against the raw data (n = 61)I recomputed the group's tests from the response file. Social media use was significantly related to sleep quality. It was not significantly related to academic performance or well-being, and there was no significant gender difference.

Scores combine related survey questions. For sleep, a higher score means poorer sleep. A correlation shows an association, not cause and effect. The group report's conclusion described the academic and well-being results as supported, but the tests show they are not significant, and this page follows the tests.

My interpretation

For a brand targeting students, this sample points to entertainment-led, short-form content on Instagram and TikTok, which together are the main platform for 89% of respondents. This is my reading of descriptive results. The survey did not test marketing effectiveness.

Limits: self-reported answers, one university, a low-reliability academic performance scale (α = 0.57), convenience sampling, and a cross-sectional design that cannot show cause and effect.

This research project gave me a better understanding of how data can be used to investigate questions about student behaviour rather than relying on assumptions. Working with survey findings and statistical analysis highlighted the importance of interpreting results carefully, distinguishing statistically significant relationships from unsupported conclusions, and acknowledging the limitations of a study. I also learned that research findings need to be presented accurately and in context, especially when exploring relationships between social media use, academic performance, well-being, and sleep.
